Project Scope

This research project documented the living botanical knowledge of Austin's diverse communities through in-depth interviews with elders, gardeners, healers, and community members. The project prioritized Indigenous, Latinx, Asian, and other marginalized voices often absent from mainstream botanical documentation.

Communities Engaged:

  • Coahuiltecan descendants (Indigenous to Central Texas)
  • Mexican-American families (multi-generational East Austin residents)
  • Vietnamese community (East Austin)
  • Honduran and Salvadoran immigrants
  • African American gardeners (East Austin)
  • Korean families
  • Ethiopian community
  • Long-time Anglo residents with traditional knowledge

Research Methodology

Participatory community-based research approach

Ethical Framework

This project was conducted using Indigenous research methodologies that prioritize community benefit, reciprocal relationships, and cultural protocol. All interviews were conducted with full informed consent, and participants maintain ownership of their stories.

Key Principles:

  • Community Ownership: Stories belong to the communities and individuals who shared them
  • Reciprocal Benefit: Research serves community needs, not just academic purposes
  • Cultural Protocol: Respects traditional ways of sharing knowledge
  • Intergenerational: Connects elders with younger community members

Data Collection Process

Phase 1: Community Engagement (6 months)

  • Relationship building with community leaders and organizations
  • Attending community events and gatherings
  • Building trust through consistent presence and reciprocal support

Phase 2: Interviews & Documentation (12 months)

  • Semi-structured interviews lasting 1-3 hours each
  • Garden visits and plant identification walks
  • Photo documentation with permission
  • Recipe and remedy collection

Phase 3: Community Verification (ongoing)

  • Story review and approval by participants
  • Community presentations and feedback sessions
  • Iterative refinement based on community input

Detailed Research Findings

Key themes and insights from 18 months of community engagement

  • Sacred Reciprocity with Plant Life Indigenous and traditional communities maintain active spiritual relationships with plants, emphasizing permission-asking, gratitude, and reciprocal care. This contrasts sharply with extractive approaches to botanical resources.
  • Medicinal Plant Knowledge Networks Over 30 plants were identified for medicinal use, with knowledge networks spanning families, neighborhoods, and cultural communities. Traditional healing practices persist alongside modern healthcare, often filling gaps in accessible treatment.
  • Food Sovereignty and Cultural Preservation Community gardens and backyard food production serve as crucial sites for maintaining cultural identity, with traditional crops and cooking methods preserved through gardening practice. This knowledge is increasingly threatened by gentrification and displacement.
  • Climate Adaptation Strategies Long-term residents possess sophisticated understanding of Central Texas climate patterns, with planting calendars, drought strategies, and companion planting systems adapted to local conditions over generations.
  • Intergenerational Knowledge Transfer Challenges Many communities struggle with language barriers, urban displacement, and changing lifestyles that interrupt traditional knowledge transmission. Elders expressed urgency about preserving knowledge before it's lost.
  • Plant-Based Spiritual and Ceremonial Practices Multiple communities maintain active spiritual relationships with plants through ceremony, daily ritual, and seasonal observances. These practices often remain private within communities but are central to cultural continuity.
  • Community Organizing Through Gardening Gardens serve as organizing spaces for communities to address food access, environmental justice, and cultural preservation. They create networks of mutual support and resource sharing.
  • Adaptation and Innovation Immigrant communities demonstrate remarkable adaptability, identifying local plants that serve similar purposes to traditional remedies, creating new fusion approaches to gardening and healing.

Recommendations for Further Research

  • Expand documentation to include Central Texas rural communities
  • Develop educational materials for broader community sharing
  • Create mentorship programs connecting elders with young gardeners
  • Document seasonal harvesting and preparation techniques through video
  • Establish seed-saving networks to preserve heritage varieties
