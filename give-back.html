Essentials Creative · a reciprocity installation, in development

Give
Back

Land Back. Water Back. Data Back. Three literal cycles of return — not a picture of one. In development for Austin, San Antonio, and the I-35 corridor between them.

01

Does it give
back more
than it takes?

It's the rule we hold every technology in the room to — the same test you take at the door. Fail it, and it doesn't belong in the room.

02

The threshold · you give first

You enter
by giving

A handful of soil. A word. A pour of water. In return the room gives back first: an anonymous token in your hand — no name, no email, no trace.

03

Movement one

Land
Back

Press the soil into a living bed. A small, bodily act of return to the ground — kneeling, pressing, leaving a mark the next visitor builds on.

04

Movement two

Water
Back

Pour your water into the reeds. It moves through a living phytoremediation loop — plants cleaning water — and comes back measurably clearer. Real ecology, not a video of cleaning. Here, water means the Edwards and Trinity aquifers: the springs the Payaya named Yanaguana.

05

Movement three · the core test

Data Back

Data is the hardest of the three to give back — soil and water want to return; data is built to be kept. So the whole framework is proven or broken here. Give a word. Watch it join a projection field woven with others'. Then choose:

  • Take it— print it, carry it out
  • Hear it— read back into the room
  • Gift it— the only path to retention
  • Erase it— deleted on screen, in front of you

Here, deletion is the norm — the opposite of every other screen. Retention is the exception you choose.

06

You leave
receiving

The loop the threshold opened closes. You gave first; the room gave back. On the way out, a readout tallies the day — power made, power used, power returned — energy as the fourth thing given back alongside soil, water, and data.

The work follows research-as-ceremony — offering before taking — and Indigenous data-sovereignty principles (CARE and OCAP®): deletion by default, retention only by consent. We make this work from our own Indigenous, Latinx, and Asian heritages.

All of this sits along I-35 between Austin and San Antonio — one of the fastest-growing data-center markets in the country. The buildout is landing in Hutto, Taylor, Round Rock, and Caldwell County, drawing on the same land, water, power, and data the work returns.
